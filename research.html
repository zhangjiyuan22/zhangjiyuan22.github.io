The missing quadrant in planet demographics

Planet occurrence is known to depend on host-star metallicity, planet mass, and orbital period. Yet the occurrence-metallicity relation remains unmeasured for low-mass planets beyond the snow line—the regime where microlensing is uniquely sensitive. Measuring this relation would test how the solid reservoir in protoplanetary disks controls the formation of cold planets, and could reveal the low-metallicity threshold below which even low-mass planet formation is suppressed.

Roman will discover planets across the Galactic disk and bulge, extending this demographic measurement beyond the solar neighborhood. The obstacle is that its lens stars will often be faint, distant M dwarfs blended with the source, making conventional metallicity measurements unavailable.

New measurement capability

Photometric–microlensing metallicity

I developed a method that makes metallicity identifiable by combining three complementary pieces of information:

  1. measures optical–near-infrared colors that are sensitive to metallicity in cool M dwarfs.
  2. Microlensing geometryprovides the mass–distance constraint needed to infer the lens absolute magnitude.
  3. Stellar physics and reddening geometryseparate intrinsic color from extinction and jointly constrain mass, distance, extinction, and metallicity.

The method can be applied homogeneously to planetary and non-planetary microlensing events, providing both the detected hosts and the parent lens sample required for an occurrence calculation. With Roman's planned survey and additional late-time F062 imaging, it could enable the first occurrence–metallicity measurement for cold low-mass planets, constrain their low-metallicity cutoff, and extend these studies into the inner Galaxy.

The missing architectures of cold planetary systems

Kepler and radial-velocity surveys have revealed that planetary systems are often multiple and highly structured, but their strongest demographic constraints concern relatively warm or massive planets. The occurrence and architecture of systems containing low-mass planets beyond the snow line remain largely unknown.

Triple-lens microlensing can probe both two planets orbiting a single host and planets residing in stellar binaries. Roman is expected to expand this sample to of order one hundred events. Turning those detections into reliable population measurements, however, requires systematic inference across the full, highly multimodal triple-lens parameter space.

New computational capability

AdaMGrid

A conventional search recalculates the same underlying magnification pattern along every trial source trajectory. AdaMGrid changes the computational model:

  1. Precompute onceconstruct a magnification representation for each lens configuration.
  2. Refine adaptivelyconcentrate numerical calculations near caustics and other high-gradient regions while controlling interpolation error.
  3. Reuse at scaleevaluate many trajectories by interpolation and reuse maps across optimizer steps and different events.

This reformulation makes a previously prohibitive full-parameter search computationally feasible. Applied systematically to Roman's triple-lens sample, it would support robust recovery of degenerate solutions and population-level studies of planetary multiplicity and planets in stellar binaries in the cold, low-mass regime.

The common thread

From a survey catalog to a test of planet formation

Metallicity and multiplicity probe complementary parts of the formation process: the material available to build planets and the architectures that emerge from that process. Together, the two methods are designed to turn Roman's cold-planet census into new measurements of how planet formation depends on host composition and system architecture across the Galaxy.
